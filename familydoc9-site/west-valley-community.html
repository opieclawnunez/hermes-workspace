Heat Season Is Here — What You Need to Know

The West Valley is one of the hottest metro areas in the country. In 2024, Phoenix recorded 113 consecutive days of 100°F+ temperatures — the longest streak ever. And the human cost is real: Maricopa County reported 427 heat-related deaths in 2025, down from over 600 in each of the two prior years, but still far too many for a preventable problem.

Even in March 2026, when temperatures hit 105°F — a record for the month — the county was already investigating dozens of possible heat-related deaths. Heat season is no longer just June through August. It starts earlier and lasts longer than it used to.

Practical heat safety for West Valley residents

Hydration
  • Drink water before you feel thirsty — by the time you're thirsty, you're already behind
  • Aim for at least 8–12 cups per day; more if you're active or outdoors
  • Add electrolytes (a pinch of salt, or a sports drink) during prolonged outdoor activity
  • Avoid excessive caffeine and alcohol — both increase fluid loss
  • Check your urine color: pale yellow means you're hydrated; dark yellow means drink more
Best times to be outdoors
  • Exercise and outdoor activity: before 7 AM or after 7 PM during summer months
  • Midday (10 AM – 4 PM): stay indoors or in shade; this is when heat illness spikes
  • Pavement test: if you can't hold your hand on the asphalt for 7 seconds, it's too hot for your dog's paws — and too hot for extended outdoor activity
Walking your dogs in the heat
  • Walk early morning or after sunset — never midday in summer
  • Bring water for both of you
  • Watch for signs of overheating: excessive panting, drooling, stumbling, bright red gums
  • If a dog shows signs of heatstroke, move to shade, offer cool (not cold) water, wet their paws and belly, and call a vet
  • Short-nosed breeds (bulldogs, pugs) and older dogs are at higher risk

Who's most at risk

Heat doesn't affect everyone equally. The people most vulnerable include:

  • Older adults (65+): the body's ability to regulate temperature declines with age
  • People experiencing homelessness: limited access to shade, water, and air conditioning
  • Outdoor workers: construction, landscaping, delivery — anyone whose job keeps them in the sun
  • People with chronic conditions: heart disease, diabetes, obesity, and mental health conditions all increase risk
  • Children under 5: they heat up faster and can't always communicate how they feel
  • Pets: dogs and cats are susceptible to heatstroke, especially in cars and on hot pavement

Cooling resources in the West Valley

Maricopa County operates a Heat Relief Network with cooling centers, water stations, and refuge locations across the Valley. The network typically runs from May 1 through September 30.

  • Find a cooling center: Maricopa County Heat Relief Network
  • Water and refuge sites: many libraries, community centers, and faith organizations participate
  • Need transportation? Some sites offer shuttle service — call 211 for information

If you know someone who is elderly, lives alone, or doesn't have air conditioning, check on them. A simple phone call or visit during a heat wave can save a life.

Mental Health Resources — West Valley

Mental health is community health. The West Valley has growing resources for people dealing with stress, anxiety, depression, substance use, and crisis situations.

Crisis support (available 24/7)
  • 988 Suicide & Crisis Lifeline: call or text 988 — free, confidential, 24/7
  • Crisis Text Line: text HOME to 741741
  • EMPACT Crisis Line (West Valley): (480) 784-1500 — local, 24/7

Local mental health and counseling services

  • Coppa Health — behavioral health resources and navigation support: copahealth.org/resources
  • Maricopa County Behavioral Health: outpatient services, substance use treatment, crisis care — maricopa.gov
  • 211 Arizona: dial 2-1-1 or visit 211arizona.org to find local resources for food, housing, mental health, and more
  • Banner Behavioral Health: (602) 254-4357 — inpatient and outpatient mental health and substance use services
  • Copper Springs Behavioral Health (Avondale): (480) 565-3035 — DBT, relapse prevention, mental health diagnosis
  • Destiny Springs Healthcare (Surprise): (623) 233-3000 — outpatient mental health and peer support

Support for caregivers and families

Caregiver burnout is real, and it's common. If you're caring for an aging parent, a child with special needs, or a family member with a chronic illness, your own health matters too. Respite care, support groups, and counseling can make a meaningful difference. Start by talking to your primary care provider or calling 211 to find local options.

Local Health News & Notes

Maricopa County heat deaths decline — but the danger isn't over

Maricopa County recorded 427 heat-related deaths in 2025, a roughly 30% decline from 2024 (608 deaths) and 2023 (645 deaths). Public health officials credit the Heat Relief Network, increased public awareness, and better coordination between agencies. But 427 deaths is still 427 too many — and experts warn that climate change is pushing Arizona into "uncharted territory" with longer, more intense heat seasons.

Opioid settlement funding directed to West Valley youth programs

Maricopa County recently invested $2 million in opioid settlement funds to expand adolescent outpatient chemical dependency programs in the West Valley, including a new youth program (ages 13–17) that integrates recovery and peer support. A sign that local investment in substance use treatment is growing — but demand still outpaces supply.

What to watch: air quality and respiratory health

West Valley air quality can deteriorate during dust storms (haboobs), wildfire smoke events, and winter inversions. People with asthma, COPD, or other respiratory conditions should monitor air quality indexes and limit outdoor activity on poor-air days. AirNow.gov provides real-time local readings.
