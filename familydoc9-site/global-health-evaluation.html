How global health improves with context

Global health data can feel abstract. But behind every number is a community making decisions with imperfect information — and getting better at it.

28|

Core message

29|

Global health progress depends less on grand gestures and more on local adaptation — understanding what a specific community actually needs and can sustain.

30|
31|
32| 33|
34|
35|

When we hear "global health," we tend to think of large organizations, big budgets, and faraway places. But the most meaningful improvements often happen at the village or neighborhood level — where someone figures out how to make a proven intervention actually work with the people who live there.

36| 37| 41| 42|

Why data alone isn't enough

43|

Health data tells us what's happening — disease rates, mortality, access gaps. But data by itself doesn't fix anything. The question is always: what do we do with it? And that answer depends entirely on context.

44|

A vaccination campaign that works in one region might fail in another — not because the vaccine is different, but because the trust networks, transportation, language, and local leadership are different. The intervention is the same. The implementation is everything.

45| 46|

The mobile health revolution

47|

One of the most practical shifts in global health has been the rise of mobile health — using basic cell phones to bridge gaps that used to require physical infrastructure.

48|
    49|
  • Appointment reminders via text message have improved follow-up rates in multiple countries.
    • 50|
  • Community health workers using smartphone apps can triage, record, and refer patients in areas with no clinic nearby.
    • 51|
  • Telemedicine connects rural patients to specialists hundreds of miles away — no travel required.
    • 52|
53|

None of this requires cutting-edge technology. It requires a phone signal and someone on the other end who knows the community.

54| 55|

Local adaptation: the missing ingredient

56|

The history of global health is littered with well-funded programs that didn't last. The reason is almost always the same: they were designed for a community, not with one.

57|

Local adaptation means:

58|
    59|
  • Asking what the community already does well — and building on it
    • 60|
  • Using local languages, local leaders, and local communication styles
    • 61|
  • Designing programs that can survive after the outside funding ends
    • 62|
  • Measuring what matters to the people involved, not just what looks good in a report
    • 63|
64| 65|
66| A simple way to think about it 67|

Global health works best when it's not "global" at all — when it's hyper-local, community-driven, and built to last beyond the grant cycle.

68|
69| 70|

What this means closer to home

71|

You don't have to work for the WHO to apply these principles. The same ideas show up in community health centers, school-based clinics, and local public health departments — including here in Maricopa County.

72|

When a clinic offers evening hours because working families can't come during the day, that's local adaptation. When a health department uses Spanish-language outreach because that's what the neighborhood speaks, that's context. When a school partners with a food bank because hungry kids can't learn, that's understanding the real problem.

73| 74|

The data gap that still matters

75|

Despite real progress, significant data gaps remain. Many countries — and many communities within the US — still lack reliable health surveillance. We don't count what we don't measure, and we don't fix what we don't count.

76|

Maricopa County's own heat surveillance system is a good local example: it took years of advocacy before heat-related deaths were systematically tracked. Now that they are, the data drives real policy — cooling centers, public awareness campaigns, and targeted outreach to vulnerable populations.

77| 78|
79| Bottom line 80|

Global health isn't about saving the world from a distance. It's about understanding what a specific group of people needs, meeting them where they are, and building something that lasts. That's as true in Phoenix as it is in Port-au-Prince.
